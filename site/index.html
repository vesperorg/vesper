Vesper · VESP · the evening-star coin

Private by default.
Provable on demand.

Vesper is a cryptocurrency project built around one thing, privacy, on its own new blockchain, not a token on someone else's, with the aim of outdoing both Monero and Zcash. No transaction ever names who paid or who was paid. Every payment lands at a one-time key only its owner can recognise, its amount is sealed so the chain can check the books without reading them, and its sender is hidden among 32 possible spenders, twice Monero's crowd. Privacy is never optional, which is where Zcash went wrong. And if you ever need to prove your own history, to an accountant or a court, a view key shows yours and nobody else's.

Why we are doing this

Nothing to buy. Nothing for sale. Not yet even a coin worth anything.

First it has to be as secure and as private as we can make it. This is not a joke and not a game. We want real privacy in our money and our transactions, the kind cash used to give and no longer can.

Are you also sick of being asked where you got it and where you are sending it, as if paying a friend put you under investigation? People are entitled to their privacy. Cash cannot always do the job, least of all with someone on the other side of the world. What is needed is a modern, legitimate private currency, built in the open and tested in the open. Vesper is meant to be that.

Which is exactly why there is no sale and no price. Test coins have no value, the code is unaudited, and the honest state of its privacy is written plainly further down this page and on Breaking Vesper. Privacy that is claimed is worthless; privacy that is measured and attacked in public is the only kind worth having.

The problem

Most blockchains are the most public ledger ever built.

On Bitcoin and Ethereum every payment is written down forever, in the open, with both addresses attached. Give an analyst one address and they can walk your whole financial life: who pays you, who you pay, how much, when. Companies sell exactly that service.

Two coins fixed it, each with a flaw. Monero hides the sender in a ring of 16, the real output and 15 decoys, a small crowd that statistics have cracked before. Zcash has stronger math but made privacy optional, so almost nobody used it and the chain leaked anyway. Both still reveal which computer first announced a transaction.

Vesper sets out to beat both. It takes the best idea from each, mandatory privacy and a hidden sender from Monero, strong proofs from Zcash, adds the two things they lack, a bigger crowd for every spend and a way to prove your own history on demand, and is written in a few thousand lines of Python, with libsodium doing the curve arithmetic, so you can read every trick line by line. Where it stands against that aim today is stated plainly further down: Monero's design with twice its ring, not yet more.

How a payment hides

As many addresses as you like, a new key every time.

A Vesper address is two public keys made from two secret numbers, and one wallet hands out as many addresses as it likes, none linkable to another. The values below are from a real run of demo.py on node v0.9.0 at testnet-1 settings.

4 · Bob, and his accountant

Bob's view key finds both instantly. His accountant, holding only the view key, sees the same two payments and can spend neither.

accountant can spend: False

The whole trick is two lines of arithmetic on an elliptic curve. The sender computes the one-time key from Bob's public keys D and C; Bob recomputes it from his private view key, because a·R equals r·C. Spending needs the private spend key as well.

P = H(r·C)·G + D     (sender builds the one-time key)
x = H(a·R) + b + m      (only Bob can derive the key that spends it; m is the per-address offset, 0 for his first address)

Give someone a and B and they can list your incoming payments forever and never move a coin. That is the view key, and it is the part Monero never made comfortable.

Someone spent one of 32 possible outputs and made two sealed outputs. The chain verified three things without learning which output, who, or how much: the ring signature (one of the 32 is really theirs), the spent marker (never seen before), and that sealed inputs equal sealed outputs plus the fee. Known limit: a crowd of 32 is twice Monero's, not the whole chain.

Each layer has a price on the node: about 4 ms per payment with only receivers hidden, 8.4 with amounts sealed, 18.6 with senders hidden in a ring of 16, back to 15.1 with one joint range proof per payment, and 23 at the ring of 32 the testnet runs. Ring signatures cannot be batch-checked, which is one reason the research target matters: a proof of membership among every output has to beat the ring's per-input cost to ship.

Roadmap

What is hidden, phase by phase.

Done

1. Stealth addresses and view keys

No address ever appears on the chain. Wallets find their coins by scanning. View keys audit without spending.

Done

2. Hidden amounts

Amounts are sealed in commitments with one 736-byte range proof per payment, batch-checked per block. Nodes prove inputs equal outputs and nothing is negative without seeing a number. Every payment has at least two shuffled outputs.

Done, stage one

3. Hidden senders

Every input is signed as one of a ring of 32 outputs, twice Monero's, with a one-way spent marker that makes double spends impossible without ever naming the output. Monero's strength. Stage two, hiding among every output on the chain, is research with no design chosen yet. 44 self-test checks across all phases.

Done, measured

4. Network privacy

A payment travels quietly from node to node before anyone broadcasts it, so the first announcer is a relay, not the sender. Measured in a 200-node simulation: an attacker with 10% of the nodes names the sender's node 11% of the time, 25% without the stem. Nothing between nodes is encrypted; one flag sends everything through Tor.

Read this

A learning chain. Not money.

Vesper has no value, is not listed anywhere, and never will be. Testnet-1's tVESP are test coins, worth nothing by design. It exists so a person can read exactly how private money works and then try to do it better.

Homemade privacy cryptography is unaudited. The real projects spent years and several audits getting this right, and their first versions still had traceability bugs. If you want private money for real, use Monero.

What the chain still shows an observer today, stated plainly: that the sender is one of 32 outputs rather than one of all of them (an attacker who knows how people spend names the real one 3.0% of the time; fair would be 3.1%), the fee, the amount of each mining reward, the count and size of inputs and outputs, and that the outputs of one payment belong together. Off the chain, the first broadcast of your payment comes from a relay rather than your node, but an attacker running a share of the nodes still guesses your node about that share of the time, your peers see your address unless you run through Tor, and a dishonest node could feed your wallet decoys of its choosing, so for real privacy you run your own node, the same rule Monero users live by. The self-test includes a check that lists exactly that, so the claim on this page and the code never drift apart. The honest line today: Monero's design with twice its ring, which is still a crowd of 32 and not of everyone. "Untraceable" waits for stage two of phase 3 and an outside audit.

Run it

Ten minutes on your own PC.

Python 3.10 or newer and pip install -r requirements.txt. Everything lives in one folder. python coin.py reset-chain starts a fresh chain and keeps your wallets. A coin can be spent, or used as a decoy, only once it is 10 blocks old, so a new chain needs 32 such coins, about 42 blocks or ten to twenty minutes, before its first payment: there is nothing to hide among before that. On testnet-1 your node finds the network through the seed node in config.json; the coins are tVESP and worth nothing.

  1. selftest.bat runs 44 checks, including that the miner's address never appears on the chain, that amounts stay sealed, that a spent output is never named, that the first broadcast of a payment is not the sender's, and that a view key can see but not spend.
  2. wallet.bat creates your wallet, two secret numbers behind one password, and shows your tvsp2… testnet-1 address and a 55-character recovery code. Write the code on paper: it is the wallet, and anyone who sees it can spend.
  3. start_node.bat starts the node, explorer and miner at http://127.0.0.1:8880. Every reward lands at a different one-time key.
  4. explorer.bat opens the explorer. There is no address search. There is nothing to search.
  5. wallet.bat again shows your balance, found by reading the blocks it has not seen yet and opening the outputs that are yours. That scan is what privacy costs.
  6. testdrive.bat runs the whole test drive by itself: two throwaway nodes, a payment each way, a search of the raw chain, five break-it attempts, about five minutes.
python coin.py send tvspTHEIRADDRESS 12.5     # pay someone; change comes back to you unlinkably
python coin.py wallet new-address --label carol   # one more address, for one payer; nobody can link it to the others
python coin.py wallet export-view            # write a view-key file: can see, cannot spend
python coin.py watch data\wallets\default.viewkey.json   # what an accountant with your view key sees
python coin.py proof pay TXID                # prove one payment to one person, and nothing else
python node.py --proxy socks5h://127.0.0.1:9050   # every connection to a peer through Tor
python demo.py                               # the math, step by step, with timings
